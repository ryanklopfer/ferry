# Market-sizing inputs: US out-of-network psychotherapists and psychiatrists in private practice

Scope: quantitative inputs only (no TAM/SAM/SOM built here). Every figure carries value, source, year, definition and a reliability note. Where sources disagree, both are recorded. Research date: 2026-10-08. All figures US unless stated.

Reliability shorthand used below: **[A]** primary government/peer-reviewed; **[B]** trade association or vendor-published primary data with stated method; **[C]** secondary press coverage of a primary source I could not open; **[D]** paid market-research vendor or unverified aggregator (treat as directional only).

## Key Question 1: How many US behavioral-health clinicians are there by license type?

### Takeaway
Two official BLS series give very different headcounts for the same occupations because one excludes self-employed workers: the Employment Projections/OOH series (base year 2025, includes self-employed) counts ~81k clinical/counseling psychologists, ~533k substance-abuse/behavioral-disorder/mental-health counselors, ~76k MFTs and ~139k mental-health/SUD social workers; the OEWS wage survey (May 2023, wage-and-salary only) counts only ~72k psychologists, ~63k MFTs and ~25k psychiatrists. Prescriber counts: ~41k–48k psychiatrists (AAMC vs HRSA definitions) and ~52k ANCC-certified PMH nurse practitioners. A reasonable all-license "psychotherapy-capable" universe is roughly 900k–1.0M licenses, but a large fraction of counselors and social workers are not doing outpatient psychotherapy, so the directory-derived figure of ~175k individual private-practice psychotherapy listings on Psychology Today is the most product-relevant denominator found.

### Cited Findings

**BLS Occupational Outlook Handbook / Employment Projections (base year 2025; counts jobs including self-employed) [A]**
- Psychologists total 209,400 jobs (2025): clinical & counseling psychologists 81,300; school psychologists 64,000; psychologists all other 60,000. Median pay $99,110 (May 2025). Self-employed workers = 23% of psychologists. Projected growth 6% 2025–35. — [BLS OOH Psychologists](https://www.bls.gov/ooh/life-physical-and-social-science/psychologists.htm)
- Substance abuse, behavioral disorder, and mental health counselors (SOC 21-1018; BLS folded the old 21-1014 mental-health-counselor code into this): 533,400 jobs (2025); median $59,350 (May 2025); projected +18% 2025–35 (~98,000 new jobs). Largest employers: offices of other health practitioners 20%, outpatient MH/SA centers 16%, individual & family services 15%, residential MH/SA facilities 8%, hospitals 7%. No self-employed % listed. — [BLS OOH Counselors](https://www.bls.gov/ooh/community-and-social-service/substance-abuse-behavioral-disorder-and-mental-health-counselors.htm)
- Marriage and family therapists (SOC 21-1013): 76,300 jobs (2025); median $66,940; +14% 2025–35. Largest employers: offices of other health practitioners 33%, individual & family services 23%, self-employed 11%, outpatient care centers 10%, state government 6%. — [BLS OOH MFTs](https://www.bls.gov/ooh/community-and-social-service/marriage-and-family-therapists.htm)

### Inferences
- Self-employment is a major share of the market and is invisible in OEWS: OOH psychologists 209,400 vs OEWS clinical+school+other ≈ 160k wage-and-salary; the 23% self-employed figure for psychologists is the only BLS self-employment share found for these occupations.
- The 533k counselor figure overstates the psychotherapy market: it includes substance-abuse counselors in residential/CMHC settings; only ~20% work in "offices of other health practitioners" (the NAICS where private practices sit).
- The Psychology Today denominator (175k individual listings) is the best single proxy for "private-practice psychotherapists who market themselves," but it undercounts (not all advertise there) and includes some employed clinicians.
- SimplePractice's ~250k practitioners include non-behavioral wellness professionals and group-practice seats, so it is an upper bound on solo behavioral-health software buyers, not a count of them.

### Gaps
- Could not open BLS OEWS May 2024/May 2025 per-occupation pages (site restructured to an interactive viewer); May 2023 is the latest OEWS table captured here.
- No national ANCC-vs-AANP reconciliation for PMHNP count (52k vs ~25k); APNA's ANCC-based 52,176 is the most recent primary-sourced figure.
- No current national LPC/LMHC license count from ACA; 2020 is the newest year found.
- HRSA 2025 Behavioral Health Workforce Brief exists (projects 2038 shortages for all occupations) but the PDF download returned HTML; its supply table was not captured. — [HRSA brief URL](https://bhw.hrsa.gov/sites/default/files/bureau-health-workforce/data-research/Behavioral-Health-Workforce-Brief-2025.pdf)

## Key Question 2: What share of each license type is in private practice (solo/small group) vs employed?

### Takeaway
No single national survey covers all license types. Best available: psychologists ~45% in independent practice (APA 2016 member survey) and 23% self-employed (BLS 2025); clinical social workers 26% self-employed in private solo/group practice or as independent contractors (ASWB 2024); MFTs 33% in offices of other health practitioners + 11% self-employed (BLS 2025); counselors 20% in offices of other health practitioners (BLS 2025); psychiatrists 31.9% solo (AMA 2022, highest of any specialty) with 42.2% of all physicians in private practice in 2024.

### Cited Findings
- Psychologists: APA 2016 member survey — 45% reported an independent practice, 13% academia, ~6% schools (as reported by Pennsylvania Psychological Association). PPA member primary-setting share in independent practice: 56% (1996), 53% (2017), 59% (2018). — [PPA Pennsylvania Psychologist, 2019 (PDF)](https://assets.noviams.com/novi-file-uploads/ppa/PA_Psychologists/2019/4_2019.pdf)
- Psychologists: APA Center for Workforce Studies (2010 data, secondary): 34% self-employed, mainly private practice. — [PAR blog citing APA CWS](https://blog.parinc.com/Blog/Tag/union)
- Psychologists: Vermont 2022 licensure census — independent solo practice is the most common main setting, 54.5% of doctoral-level and 64.3% of master's-level psychologists (single state). — [Vermont Dept of Health 2022 (PDF)](https://HealthVermont.Gov/sites/default/files/document/HSI-stats-prov-psy22-report.pdf)
- Psychologists: BLS — 23% self-employed (2025); OEWS wage-and-salary clinical psychologists in Offices of Other Health Practitioners 28,960 of 71,730 (40%, my arithmetic) plus 6,720 in Offices of Physicians (May 2023). — [BLS OOH Psychologists](https://www.bls.gov/ooh/life-physical-and-social-science/psychologists.htm); [BLS OEWS 19-3033](https://www.bls.gov/oes/2023/may/oes193033.htm)
- Clinical social workers: "as high as 26% of Clinical social workers were self-employed in private sole or group practices or were working as independent contractors"; 31% hold multiple jobs; clinical SWs more likely to work part-time/part-year; 26% work primarily online. — [ASWB 2024 Report 2 (PDF)](https://www.aswb.org/wp-content/uploads/2025/07/Social-Work-Workforce-Study-Series-Report-2.pdf)
- LCSWs, Indiana 2024: 30.6% report private practice as primary setting (largest single setting). — [Indiana 2024 LCSW snapshot](https://scholarworks.indianapolis.iu.edu/items/6c265dc7-0a2f-4b62-9926-6ca9a02e9d12)
- LMHCs, Indiana 2024: private practice 43.9%, CMHC 11.6%, telehealth 11.2%. — [Indiana 2024 workforce snapshot (PDF)](https://scholarworks.indianapolis.iu.edu/server/api/core/bitstreams/1a86d6a7-c6eb-4a62-a877-e4af3f6b9eb8/content)
- MFTs: offices of other health practitioners 33%, self-employed 11% (BLS 2025). Counselors: offices of other health practitioners 20% (BLS 2025). — [BLS OOH MFTs](https://www.bls.gov/ooh/community-and-social-service/marriage-and-family-therapists.htm); [BLS OOH Counselors](https://www.bls.gov/ooh/community-and-social-service/substance-abuse-behavioral-disorder-and-mental-health-counselors.htm)
- Psychiatrists: AMA Physician Practice Benchmark Survey (2022 wave, n=3,500): psychiatrists most likely of any specialty to be in solo practice, 31.9%; all-physician private-practice share fell 60.1% (2012) → 46.7% (2022). — [DecisionHealth on AMA 2022](https://pbn.decisionhealth.com/Blogs/Detail.aspx?id=200568)
- Psychiatrists/all physicians: AMA 2024 survey (n=5,000, Aug–Sept 2024, 43% response): 42.2% of physicians in private practice (down from 60.1% in 2012); ~12% of all physicians solo in 2024 vs 18% in 2012 (all-specialty, secondary). — [AMA Benchmark Survey page](https://www.ama-assn.org/about/physician-practice-benchmark-survey); [Primary Care Companion](https://www.psychiatrist.com/pcc/practicing-resource-limited-siloed-settings/)
- Psychiatrists in NAMCS 2007–2016 (Carlo et al. 2023): solo practice = 32% weighted (33% unweighted) of psychiatrist respondents; solo psychiatrists significantly less likely to accept private (AOR 0.90), public (AOR 0.76) or any insurance (AOR 0.83) than group-practice psychiatrists. — [Carlo et al., Psychiatric Services 2023](https://psychiatryonline.org/doi/10.1176/appi.ps.202100669); [open preprint PDF](https://mmhpi.org/wp-content/uploads/2023/07/Acceptance-of-Insurance-by-Psychiatrists-and-Other-Physicians.pdf)
- Psychiatrists OEWS May 2023: Offices of Physicians 6,780 of 24,830 wage-and-salary (27%); hospitals (general + psychiatric) 7,740 (31%). Self-employed excluded. — [BLS OEWS 29-1223](https://www.bls.gov/oes/2023/may/oes291223.htm)
- Therapists in private practice (Heard 2025 report, 2024 data): 68% see fewer than 20 clients/week; "just over half" say their practice is a full-time position. — [BHB on Heard 2025 report](https://bhbusiness.com/2025/04/07/despite-the-rise-in-therapist-income-reimbursement-woes-continue/)

### Inferences
- Applying the setting shares to the 2025 BLS counts gives a rough private-practice pool: psychologists ~81k × 45% ≈ 37k; MFTs 76k × 44% (33% offices + 11% self-employed) ≈ 34k; MH/SUD social workers 139k × 26% ≈ 36k (or 273k clinical licensees × 26% ≈ 71k); counselors 533k × 20% ≈ 107k (very loose; includes SUD counselors). Psychiatrists ~41–48k × 32% solo ≈ 13–15k. These are my arithmetic, not sourced figures, and a skeptic will challenge the counselor term most.
- Psychiatrists are the specialty most concentrated in solo practice and the least likely to take insurance, which makes them a disproportionately good fit relative to their small headcount.

### Gaps
- No post-2016 national APA figure for % of health service psychologists in independent practice; APA 2024 Pulse report says private-practice psychologists are less likely to take insurance but the setting split was not published in accessible coverage.
- No national ACA/counselor private-practice share; only Indiana (43.9%) and BLS industry proxy (20%).
- AMA 2024 psychiatry-specific solo share not found (only 2022: 31.9%).

## Key Question 3: What share accept no insurance or are out-of-network for most clients?

### Takeaway
Roughly one-third of US private-practice psychotherapists take no insurance (35.1% of 175k Psychology Today listings, 2023; 34% of psychologists, APA 2024), and psychiatrists have accepted private insurance at ~55% vs ~89% for other physicians (2009–10), with solo and metro psychiatrists least likely to participate. On the demand side, 17.2% of behavioral-health office visits in commercial PPO plans were out-of-network in 2017 (Milliman), and 13.4% in 2019–21 across all commercial plans (RTI), 3.5–5.4× the medical/surgical rate; psychologist visits were OON 18.2% and psychiatrist visits 15.3%.

### Cited Findings

**Clinician-side (share not in network)**
- Psychotherapists (Psychology Today scrape, Sept–Dec 2023, n=175,083): 64.9% (113,661) accepted insurance; 35.1% (61,422, derived) did not. Psychologists were 19.1% of non-accepting vs 13.8% of accepting providers. County-mean acceptance highest in ND 90.5%, NE 87.1%, IL 86.3%; lowest DC 41.0%, TN 51.5%, CA 55.4%. — [Zhu et al. 2024, Health Affairs Scholar](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11412241/)
- Psychologists (APA 2024 Practitioner Pulse Survey "Barriers to Care in a Changing Practice Environment," online, Sept 4–30 2024, n=853, released Dec 17 2024): 34% accept no insurance; of those, 48% previously participated in networks and 18% never have. Participation by payer: 58% private/commercial, 36% FFS Medicare, 26% Medicare Advantage, 16% FFS Medicaid, 18% managed Medicaid. Reasons for not participating: insufficient reimbursement 82%, administrative burden (preauth, audits) 62%, payment reliability (delays, refund demands) 52%. 53% have no openings for new patients. Private-practice psychologists less likely to take insurance than those in hospitals/medical schools/organized settings. — [Axios](https://www.axios.com/2024/12/18/psychologists-accept-health-insurance-survey); [Behavioral Health Business](https://bhbusiness.com/2024/12/18/insurance-barriers-provider-scarcity-limits-patient-access-to-psychologists/); [HealthDay](https://www.healthday.com/healthpro-news/mental-health/psychologists-id-financial-administrative-barriers-to-accepting-insurance); [Real World Health Care (Oct 2025)](https://healthwellfoundation.org/realworldhealthcare/administrative-burdens-limit-psychologists-capacity-to-address-ongoing-mental-health-needs)
- Psychologists (APA 2021 survey, secondary): 31% accepted Medicaid, 46% Medicare. — [Psychiatric News 2024](https://psychiatryonline.org/doi/10.1176/appi.pn.2024.04.4.10)
- Psychiatrists (Bishop et al., JAMA Psychiatry 2014; NAMCS 2009–2010): 55.3% of psychiatrists accepted private non-capitated insurance vs 88.7% of other physicians; Medicare 54.8% vs 86.1%; Medicaid 43.1% vs 73.0%. — [Bishop et al. 2014 (PMC)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3967759); [MDedge coverage](https://live.mdedge.com/content/psychiatrists-least-likely-specialists-accept-insurance)
- Psychiatrists (Carlo et al., Psychiatric Services 2023; NAMCS 2007–2016, avg 4,725 physicians per 2-yr block, 7% psychiatrists): non-psychiatrists participated in all networks at higher rates; gap wider for public than private insurance; psychiatrists in MSAs (AOR 0.85 private / 0.78 public / 0.83 any) and solo practice (AOR 0.90 / 0.76 / 0.83) significantly less likely to accept insurance; exact percentages are in a figure not machine-readable. — [Carlo et al. 2023](https://psychiatryonline.org/doi/10.1176/appi.ps.202100669); [preprint PDF](https://mmhpi.org/wp-content/uploads/2023/07/Acceptance-of-Insurance-by-Psychiatrists-and-Other-Physicians.pdf)
- Psychiatrists (older national survey): 85% willing to accept new patients; 77% accepted self-pay; 44% accepted Medicaid. — [Psychiatric Services 2005](https://ps.psychiatryonline.org/doi/10.1176/appi.ps.56.4.408)
- Medicare Advantage psychiatrist networks (2019 data): nearly two-thirds of MA psychiatrist networks contain <25% of psychiatrists in the service area. — [Zhu et al. via Mirage News](https://www.miragenews.com/scant-mental-health-care-coverage-for-older-1041347/)
- Reimbursement gap (therapist-reported): private pay ~$150 vs insurance ~$105 (2025, ~30% less); $159 vs $111 (2024, 36% less). — [Heard 2026 report](https://www.joinheard.com/resources/downloads/the-heard-2026-financial-state-of-private-practice-report); [BHB on Heard 2025](https://bhbusiness.com/2025/04/07/despite-the-rise-in-therapist-income-reimbursement-woes-continue/)

**Patient-side (share of visits out-of-network)**
- Milliman Nov 2019 (37M commercial PPO employees/dependents, 2013–2017): behavioral office visits OON 19.0% (2013), 19.1% (2014), 18.9% (2015), 17.9% (2016), 17.2% (2017) vs primary care 3.8/4.0/3.7/3.1/3.2% and med/surg specialists 5.1/5.1/5.2/4.3/4.3%; disparity 5.4× PCP and 4.0× specialists in 2017. Inpatient behavioral OON 9.6% (2013) → 17.2% (2017); outpatient facility behavioral OON 27.6% (2017). Child behavioral office visit 10.1× more likely OON than PCP (2017), >2× the adult disparity. SUD office visits 5.7× PCP (2013) → 9.5× (2017). In 2017 PCP in-network reimbursement was 23.8% higher than behavioral (behavioral paid 97.2% of Medicare-allowed vs PCP 120.4%, specialists 115.6%). Behavioral health spend = 2.2–2.4% of total PPO spend (MH), SUD 0.7% → 0.9%, combined incl. Rx 5.2% (2017). — [Milliman 2019 report (PDF)](https://assets.milliman.com/ektron/Addiction_and_mental_health_vs_physical_health_Widening_disparities_in_network_use_and_provider_reimbursement.pdf); [AACAP summary](https://www.aacap.org/AACAP/Latest_News/New_Milliman_Report_Confirms_Access_Crisis_to_Mental_Health_Care_Worsening)
- RTI International, April 2024 (22M+ commercially insured lives/year, 2019–2021, all 50 states; funded by Bowman Family Foundation's MHTARI with APA/APA/NABH): behavioral office visits OON 3.5× med/surg (13.4% vs 3.8%); psychiatrist visits 8.9× med/surg clinicians (15.3% vs 2.2% for PCPs = 6.9×); psychologist visits 10.6× (18.2% vs 2.2% = 8.2× PCP); acute inpatient 6.2×, sub-acute 19.9×. In-network reimbursement for med/surg office visits 22% higher on average, 48% at 75th percentile, 70% at 95th; PAs paid 19% more than psychiatrists and 23% more than psychologists. No improvement vs Milliman's 2013 baseline. — [RTI press release](https://www.rti.org/news/study-disparities-in-network-access-mental-health-sud-treatment); [Psychiatric News Alert](https://alert.psychnews.org/2024/04/access-to-in-network-mental-health-care.html); [AJMC](https://ajmc.com/view/low-reimbursement-rates-for-mental-health-care-linked-with-high-out-of-network-provider-use)

### Inferences
- Combining supply-side (~35% of directory therapists take no insurance) with demand-side (~13–17% of commercial BH office visits are OON) implies non-insurance therapists see a mix of OON-benefit clients and pure self-pay/no-benefit clients; the OON-claim-filing value proposition reaches only the subset of their clients with PPO/POS coverage.
- The OON share is strongly geographic: DC, CA, NY, TN, and other high-cost metros have 45–60% of listed therapists accepting insurance, so an OON-first product's SAM is concentrated in a dozen metros.
- Milliman's 17.2% is PPO-only (2017) and RTI's 13.4% is all commercial plan types (2019–21); the RTI figure is the better base for all commercially insured people, Milliman's for PPO enrollees.

### Gaps
- No 2025–2026 primary update to the psychiatrist insurance-acceptance rate; newest NAMCS-based data is 2015–16 (Carlo 2023).
- The APA 2024 report PDF itself was not accessible; figures above are from three independent press accounts that agree with each other.
- No source quantifies "out-of-network for most clients" as a share of clinicians (sources measure "accept no insurance" or "visits OON").

## Key Question 4: Session economics (fees, sessions/week, clients, sessions/client/year)

### Takeaway
Private-pay therapy averages ~$143–182 per session depending on source and year (directory mean $143 in 2023; Zencare $182 in 2022; Heard $150–159 in 2024–25), with psychologists ~$170–196 and major metros $175–215; insurance reimburses ~30–36% less (~$105–111). A typical private practitioner sees ~15–25 clients/week (68% see fewer than 20), and psychotherapy patients average ~10–12 visits/year (9.8 in 2018 → 11.8 in 2021). Self-pay psychiatry follow-ups run ~$115–225 (99213–99215) with intakes ~$425.

### Cited Findings

**Therapy fees (private pay)**
- Mean cash-pay rate $143.26/session (Psychology Today listings, late 2023; Table 1 mean $146.76, SD $46.00, 36.8% of listings missing a rate). ### Inferences

- Implied clients per full-time therapist: 20 weekly sessions ÷ (11.8 visits/yr ÷ 52 ≈ 0.23 visits/client/week) ≈ 85–90 unique clients/year, but active caseload at any time is closer to 20–30.
- Per-session claim economics: a $150 OON session generates one claim; at ~900 sessions/yr per full-time clinician, "free filing of every claim" means ~900 claims/yr per seat for a $600/yr subscription (≈$0.67/claim) versus SimplePractice's $1.79/claim and Upheal's $0.30/claim submission fees.

## Key Question 5: Spend on outpatient psychotherapy/psychiatry, OON/out-of-pocket share, OON claim counts, share of insured with OON benefits

### Takeaway
US mental-health spending was ~$174B/yr in 2021–23 (MEPS), of which ~$107B was ambulatory; BEA-based MH/SUD treatment spending was $139.6B in 2021. About 13–17% of commercial behavioral-health office visits are out-of-network. Among covered workers, 55% are in PPO or POS plans (which carry OON benefits) and another 33% in HDHP/SO plans (network type unspecified), while only ~14% of 2025 marketplace plans are PPOs. No source counts OON behavioral-health claims per year.

### Cited Findings

**Total and ambulatory spend**

### Inferences
- Ambulatory MH spend of ~$107B (MEPS) covers all payers and all outpatient settings (clinics, CMHCs, hospital outpatient, telehealth platforms). The private-practice psychotherapy/psychiatry slice is a fraction; a crude cross-check: ~260M psychotherapy visits × ~$120 blended ≈ $31B for psychotherapy alone.
- OON-reachable spend ≈ (commercial BH office-visit spend) × 13–17%. With commercially insured people roughly half the population and the PPO/POS share of employer enrollment at 55% (plus an unknown share of the 33% HDHP/SO that are PPO-network), the population with any OON benefit is on the order of 90–120M people; marketplace enrollees (24M) mostly lack OON benefits.
- No published count of OON behavioral-health claims exists; it must be derived (e.g., commercial BH office visits × OON share), and investors will challenge every term.

### Gaps
- The MEPS JAMA Psychiatry paper's payer/out-of-pocket breakdown is in the full text, which I could not access; only setting totals are captured.
- No source for total OON behavioral-health claims per year or for what share of OON sessions actually get a claim submitted (superbill usage rate).
- KFF does not break HDHP/SO enrollment by network type, so the true share of covered workers with OON benefits is bounded at 55%–88%.
- No OON-share data for Medicare/Medicaid (not relevant to OON claim filing but relevant to clinician payer mix).

## Cross-cutting flags for the model builder (inputs a skeptical investor will attack)

1. **Which clinician count to use.** OOH (includes self-employed) vs OEWS (excludes them) differ by 10–40% per occupation; the 533k counselor figure includes SUD counselors in institutional settings. Recommend building on OOH 2025 counts × private-practice shares, and triangulating against the 175k Psychology Today individual listings.
2. **"No insurance" ≠ "OON clients file claims."** 35% of listed therapists take no insurance, but only clients with PPO/POS (or PPO-network HDHP) coverage have anything to claim; marketplace and HMO enrollees (and Medicaid) do not. The share of a no-insurance therapist's caseload that is OON-claimable is unmeasured.
3. **Psychiatrists are small in number (~41–48k) but ~32% solo and the least likely specialty to take insurance** — a high-fit, low-count segment; PMHNPs (~52k) are growing fast but their network participation is unmeasured.
4. **Fee and caseload data come from self-selected samples** (Heard clients, Zencare listings, Psychology Today profiles with 37% missing fees). Use ranges, not point estimates.
5. **Market-research TAMs are unauditable** and mostly global; do not present them as the TAM.
6. **Oldest primary studies still in use:** Bishop 2014 (2009–10 data) and Milliman 2019 (2013–17 data). RTI 2024 (2019–21 data) and Zhu 2024 (2023 data) are the newest primaries on OON share; APA 2024 Pulse is the newest clinician survey.
