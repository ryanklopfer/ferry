# Ferry sells into 80,000 out-of-network clinicians, not millions

**Bottom line.** Ferry's realistic market is about **80,000 US private-practice therapists and psychiatrists who take little or no insurance**, inside a universe of roughly 220,000 private-practice behavioral-health clinicians. At the $50/month placeholder that is a **serviceable market (SAM) of about $48 million a year** (low $29M, high $78M) and a **total market (TAM) of about $132 million** (low $113M, high $168M). Built the other way round, from what clinicians already pay for AI notes plus out-of-network claim handling, the same wallet comes out at **$139 million TAM and $76 million SAM**, so the two methods agree within a third. A credible three-year goal is **about 3,200 paying clinicians, or $1.9 million in annual revenue** (low $0.4M, high $6.3M); that is the same clinician count the team's older revenue model assumed, but at the new $50 price it produces about 40% of the revenue that model projected. The market is expanding on the demand side (behavioral-health visits now outnumber primary-care visits among the commercially insured), the out-of-network core of psychologists and psychiatrists is growing, the AI-scribe tooling around it is mature in price and consolidating by acquisition, and no product yet combines session notes, client-payee claim filing and appeal letters under one clinician-paid fee. In four of five target practices the person who pays, the person who uses the product and the person who decides are the same clinician, which makes this a self-serve, trial-led, peer-validated purchase rather than a sales-led one. The assumptions most likely to break are the share of clients who actually hold out-of-network benefits, client consent to recording, and whether $50 covers the cost of recording a full session.

## Bottom-up: 220,000 private-practice clinicians, 80,000 of them out-of-network

The bottom-up model starts with a head count, keeps the clinicians whose clients have something to claim, and applies a penetration rate. Every row is labelled so one number can be changed and the rest recomputed.

The starting head count has no official source, so it is triangulated three ways. A 2023 scrape of the Psychology Today directory counted **175,083 individual private-practice psychotherapists** after removing group listings and psychiatrists ([Zhu et al., Health Affairs Scholar 2024](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11412241/)). Applying private-practice shares to the 2025 federal job counts gives a similar order of magnitude: about 81,300 clinical and counseling psychologists of whom roughly 45% are in independent practice, 76,300 marriage and family therapists of whom 44% work in practitioner offices or for themselves, 138,900 mental-health social workers of whom about 26% are self-employed, and 533,400 counselors of whom about 20% work in practitioner offices ([BLS OOH Psychologists](https://www.bls.gov/ooh/life-physical-and-social-science/psychologists.htm); [BLS OOH MFTs](https://www.bls.gov/ooh/community-and-social-service/marriage-and-family-therapists.htm); [BLS OOH Social Workers](https://www.bls.gov/ooh/community-and-social-service/social-workers.htm); [BLS OOH Counselors](https://www.bls.gov/ooh/community-and-social-service/substance-abuse-behavioral-disorder-and-mental-health-counselors.htm); [ASWB 2024](https://www.aswb.org/wp-content/uploads/2025/07/Social-Work-Workforce-Study-Series-Report-2.pdf); [PPA on APA 2016 survey](https://assets.noviams.com/novi-file-uploads/ppa/PA_Psychologists/2019/4_2019.pdf)). That arithmetic lands at roughly 215,000 to 250,000. The ceiling is SimplePractice, the category's biggest software vendor, which reports **"nearly 250,000" practitioners**, a figure that includes non-behavioral wellness providers and group seats ([BusinessWire, Nov 2025](https://www.businesswire.com/news/home/20251104952447/en)). The base case uses 200,000 therapists.

Psychiatry is small in number but high in fit. The AAMC counts **40,780 active psychiatrists plus 11,147 child and adolescent psychiatrists** ([Becker's on AAMC 2024](https://www.beckersasc.com/asc-news/how-1-million-active-us-physicians-break-down-across-52-specialties/)); HRSA counts 47,894 ([APNA 2025, citing HRSA](https://www.apna.org/wp-content/uploads/2025/10/APNA_2025_State_of_Psychiatric-Mental_Health_Nursing_FINAL.pdf)). Psychiatrists are the most solo of any medical specialty, at **31.9% solo in 2022** ([DecisionHealth on AMA 2022](https://pbn.decisionhealth.com/Blogs/Detail.aspx?id=200568)) or 25.6% in 2020 ([AMA 2020](https://www.ama-assn.org/system/files/2021-05/2020-prp-physician-practice-arrangements.pdf)), which gives 11,000 to 17,000 solo psychiatrists. Psychiatric nurse practitioners add about 52,000 certified clinicians, but no source says how many are in private practice ([APNA 2025](https://www.apna.org/wp-content/uploads/2025/10/APNA_2025_State_of_Psychiatric-Mental_Health_Nursing_FINAL.pdf)), so the base case adds only 5,000 of them.

The out-of-network share in row F rests on the largest sample available: **35.1% of the 175,083 listed therapists accept no insurance**, rising to 42.7% among psychologists, and the share ranges from 9.5% in North Dakota to 59% in Washington DC ([Zhu et al. 2024](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11412241/)). Heard's accounting clients are lower at **25.4% cash-pay only**, but 87.3% of its insurance-accepting practices also carry private-pay rates ([Heard 2026](https://www.joinheard.com/resources/the-heard-2026-financial-state-of-private-practice-report)). The APA's member survey puts psychologists at **38% not in any network in 2025** ([APA 2025 Topline](https://www.apa.org/pubs/reports/practitioner/2025/topline-data-tables.pdf)), and a psychologist-heavy peer-reviewed sample found 61% took no insurance ([PMC13175211](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13175211/)). For psychiatry, the standing figures are that **55.3% accepted private insurance in 2009–10 versus 88.7% of other physicians** ([Bishop et al. 2014](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3967759)) and that nearly half take no insurance at all ([PMC11014756](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11014756/)).

A base-case ramp to 3,200 paying clinicians would look like roughly 500 at the end of year one, 1,600 at the end of year two and 3,200 at the end of year three, which at $600 is $0.3M, $1.0M and $1.9M of run-rate revenue. Those are end-of-year counts net of churn, so sign-ups must run well ahead of them (pressure test 6).

## Top-down: the wallet Ferry replaces is about $950 a year per clinician

The top-down model asks what these clinicians already pay, or would pay at market prices, for the two things Ferry bundles: AI documentation and out-of-network claim handling. It deliberately ignores the paid market-research reports, which put global behavioral-health software at $3.5–5.5 billion and the US AI-scribe market at about $0.5 billion but disclose no method and never isolate solo practices ([Towards Healthcare](https://www.towardshealthcare.com/insights/us-ai-in-medical-scribing-market-sizing)); those are context, not an anchor.

The documentation wallet is well anchored. The three largest therapy EHRs sell AI notes as an add-on at **$35/month (SimplePractice Note Taker)**, **$40/month (TherapyNotes TherapyFuel)** and **$35/month (TheraNest and Sessions Health)** ([SimplePractice pricing](https://www.simplepractice.com/pricing/); [Software Advice on TherapyNotes](https://www.softwareadvice.com/resources/simplepractice-vs-therapynotes-ai-features/)), and standalone scribes cluster at $19.99–$69.99 with unlimited plans at $39 (Upheal) and $69 (Blueprint seat) ([Upheal pricing](https://www.upheal.io/pricing); [Blueprint pricing](https://www.blueprint.ai/pricing); [Mentalyc pricing](https://www.mentalyc.com/pricing)). The base case uses $450 a year. The out-of-network claim wallet is thinner and mostly paid by clients today: Mentaya charges the client 5% per claim, Thrizer 1–5%, and Reimbursify $3.99 per claim or **$53–$89 a month per practitioner** on practice-paid plans ([Mentaya pricing](https://mentaya.com/pricing); [Thrizer pricing](https://www.thrizer.com/pricing); [Reimbursify practice pricing](https://reimbursify.com/practice-pricing/)). The base case uses $500 a year of clinician-side value, roughly Reimbursify's entry plan.

Two cross-checks tie the models to real money. First, the clinicians' total software wallet: Heard's 2024 data puts average software spend at **$3,636 per therapist per year** ([Heard income article](https://www.joinheard.com/articles/how-much-money-does-a-private-practice-therapist-make)), which across 220,000 clinicians is about $800 million; Ferry's $600 is 16.5% of that wallet, and the whole bottom-up TAM is 16.5% of it. Second, claim volume: there were **66.4 million behavioral-health visits among the commercially insured in 2024** ([BHB on Trilliant](https://bhbusiness.com/2025/10/09/behavioral-health-visits-eclipsed-primary-care-visits-in-2024/)), and 13.4% to 17.2% of behavioral-health office visits are out-of-network ([RTI 2024](https://www.rti.org/news/study-disparities-in-network-access-mental-health-sud-treatment); [Milliman 2019](https://assets.milliman.com/ektron/Addiction_and_mental_health_vs_physical_health_Widening_disparities_in_network_use_and_provider_reimbursement.pdf)), so about **8.9 to 11.4 million out-of-network behavioral-health claims reach commercial insurers each year**, worth $1.3–1.7 billion at the $143 directory-average fee. That is the pool of claims Ferry would file and chase, and it carries a warning explored in the next section: 80,000 SAM clinicians at roughly 650 sessions a year deliver about 52 million sessions, so only about one session in five currently shows up as a filed commercial out-of-network claim.

The top-down SOM uses money changing hands today rather than the full wallet because a new entrant mostly takes share from existing spend; growth in scribe adoption, which rose from 20% to 29% of physicians in under a year ([Doximity 2026](https://www.doximity.com/reports/state-of-ai-medicine-report/2026)), is the upside on top of it.

Assumption 3 deserves the founder's attention most, because it is where the two halves of the product meet. Among covered workers, **46% are in PPO plans and 9% in POS plans**, the two types that reliably carry out-of-network benefits, while 33% are in high-deductible plans whose network type the survey does not break out ([KFF 2025 EHBS](https://www.kff.org/health-costs/report/2025-employer-health-benefits-survey/)); on the individual market only 14% of plans are PPOs and many of those carry "quite meager" out-of-network coverage ([RWJF Marketplace Pulse](https://www.rwjf.org/en/insights/our-research/2025/01/marketplace-pulse-preferred-provider-organizations-remain-rare-in-the-individual-market.html)). The claims cross-check above says the same thing from the other side: the number of out-of-network behavioral-health claims that actually reach commercial insurers is a fraction of the sessions the out-of-network clinician pool delivers. The honest reading is that for the typical out-of-network clinician, somewhere between one in five and one in two sessions is claimable, with the rest self-pay with no usable benefit; the claims-based studies cannot see superbills that clients never filed, so the true claimable share is higher than one in five but nobody has measured it. The value of "free filing of every claim" is therefore concentrated in metro, PPO-heavy caseloads, and the scribe has to carry the product for everyone else.

Assumption 4 hides a cost question that sizing cannot settle but must flag. The researchers recorded AWS HealthScribe's list price at **$0.001667 per second, about $0.10 per minute** ([AWS HealthScribe pricing](https://aws.amazon.com/healthscribe/pricing/)). A 50-minute recorded session is about $5 of transcription before any claim or model costs; a clinician who records 650 sessions a year costs about $3,250 against $600 of revenue, and even recording only a third of sessions costs more than the subscription. The team's own earlier model priced claim handling at $1.65 per claim, which on 150–300 claimable sessions a year is another $250–$500. Competitors selling unlimited notes at $39 presumably use cheaper transcription. This report has not verified the price or whether volume terms apply; the founder should check it against the architecture and cost documents before treating $50 as a floor.

Assumption 5 is the one the team already flagged. The evidence splits cleanly by what leads the sale: products that lead with out-of-network reimbursement have stayed small and unfunded, with Mentaya on a 2021 pre-seed, Thrizer bootstrapped with **2,500+ practitioners** ([Thrizer about](https://www.thrizer.com/company/about-us)), and SuperBill abandoning the therapist market to become a voice-AI company; products that lead with notes grew to tens of thousands, with Freed at **17,000+ paying clinicians** and an estimated $19M of annual revenue after a $30M Series A ([Freed press](https://www.getfreed.ai/press/freed-secures-30m-series-a-led-by-sequoia-capital-to-free-clinicians-from-administrative-burdens-with-ai-assistant); [Sacra on Freed](https://sacra.com/research/freed-at-19m-arr/)). Whether Ferry reaches 3,200 depends on whether it is bought as a scribe that happens to file claims, not as a claims tool that happens to write notes.

## The demand is expanding, the OON core is growing, the tooling is consolidating

The verdict differs by layer, and the layers matter more than the headline.

Highest fees, most claimable clients, growing in number |

| Regulation | Tightening on recording, loosening on parity | Illinois (Aug 2025), Rhode Island, Louisiana and Colorado (Aug 2026) consent laws; the 2024 federal parity rule is unenforced and will be rewritten, with no replacement before 2027 | Consent must be a first-class feature; no policy tailwind will push clients back in-network soon |

Demand is the easiest call. Trilliant's 2026 report shows **behavioral-health utilization up 62.6% between 2018 and 2024** with a national provider adequacy rate of only 27.3% ([Trilliant 2026](https://www.trillianthealth.com/market-research/reports/2026-behavioral-health-report)), and the 2024 federal parity rule that might have forced insurers to improve in-network access is now under a non-enforcement policy that the agencies have told a court they will replace rather than defend ([DOL statement](https://www.dol.gov/agencies/ebsa/laws-and-regulations/laws/mental-health-parity/statement-regarding-enforcement-of-the-final-rule-on-requirements-related-to-mhpaea); [NFP on the 2026 status](https://www.nfp.com/insights/departments-signal-redo-on-mhpaea-regulations/)). Nothing in policy will shrink out-of-network demand before 2027.

The supply picture is a split, not a trend. The APA's longitudinal series shows **psychologists in any network falling from 66% to 62% in one year** and **10% of in-network psychologists dropping a commercial panel in the prior twelve months** ([APA 2025 Topline](https://www.apa.org/pubs/reports/practitioner/2025/topline-data-tables.pdf)), while SimplePractice's user data shows **insurance-billed sessions rising from 56% to 59%** ([BusinessWire, May 2026](https://secure.businesswire.com/news/home/20260513758800/en/SimplePractice-Releases-First-of-Its-Kind-Report-on-Independent-Mental-Health-Practice)). Both are true: the platforms make taking insurance cheap for master's-level clinicians, who dominate SimplePractice, while doctoral-level and psychiatric clinicians keep leaving. The out-of-network pool is becoming more concentrated in higher-fee practices, and the in-network platforms have grown to a size larger than the whole out-of-network pool without visibly draining it; Headway reports **65,000+ providers** ([Headway 2025 report](https://headway.co/blog/annual-report-2025)) and Grow Therapy reports about $1 billion of revenue at a reported $3 billion valuation ([BHB, Mar 2026](https://bhbusiness.com/2026/03/03/with-1b-in-revenue-grow-therapy-lands-150m-series-d/)).

The tooling verdict shapes differentiation. Three events in six months, Grow buying the Tenor scribe ([BHB, Feb 2026](https://bhbusiness.com/2026/02/10/grow-therapy-buys-ai-scribe-tool-tenor-therapy/)), Headway acquiring Tezi's team ([Fierce Healthcare](https://www.fiercehealthcare.com/health-tech/mental-health-provider-platform-headway-acquires-team-behind-ai-company-tezi)) and Spring Health closing on Alma ([HIT Consultant](https://hitconsultant.net/2026/05/01/spring-health-alma-acquisition-lifelong-mental-health/)), confirm that the in-network platforms are converging on "notes plus billing, free to the clinician, paid by payers." Headway's scribe is free but limited to its own telehealth sessions and excludes intake, in-person and psychiatric sessions ([Headway Scribe help](https://help.headway.co/hc/en-us/articles/45361340461076-AI-assisted-notes-Notes-generated-from-Headway-Telehealth-transcript-with-Scribe)). That free alternative is only available to clinicians willing to accept platform rates, which Optum cut by up to $43 a visit at Headway and Alma in late 2024 ([BHB, Dec 2024](https://bhbusiness.com/2024/12/19/recent-optum-behavioral-health-rate-cuts-bring-to-life-harsh-reality-of-the-payer-provider-complex/)). The out-of-network tools, by contrast, are so lightly funded that the market has judged standalone claim filing a small business; bundling it as the reason to pick one scribe over another is the plausible play, and no competitor has done it.

For timing, late 2026 is a window with a closing edge. Ambient scribing has crossed into the mainstream, with about 60 vendors and typical adoption of 20–50% where offered ([PHTI 2025](https://phti.org/wp-content/uploads/sites/3/2025/03/PHTI-Adoption-of-AI-in-Healthcare-Delivery-Systems-Early-Applications-Impacts.pdf)) and **psychologists reporting monthly AI use up from 11% to 29% in a year** ([APA Monitor](https://www.apa.org/monitor/2026/03/ai-reshaping-therapy)), so clinicians no longer need convincing that a scribe is legitimate, only which one to pick. The closing edge is law: Illinois now requires separate explicit written consent before AI records or transcribes a therapy session, with penalties up to $10,000 per violation ([Holland & Knight](https://www.hklaw.com/en/insights/publications/2025/08/new-illinois-law-restricts-use-of-ai-in-mental-health-therapy)), and Colorado, Rhode Island and Louisiana followed in 2026 ([psychology.com state register](https://psychology.com/ce/guides/ai-therapy-laws-by-state/)). A product launched now with per-client, state-aware consent built in is ahead of that wave; one launched later will be entering a market where consent compliance is table stakes and the big EHRs have caught up.

For differentiation, the evidence points to three choices. Lead with the out-of-network core of psychologists and psychiatrists, where fees are highest, insurance acceptance lowest and the free platform alternative weakest. Sell the claim loop as the reason to choose this scribe, since notes themselves are priced like a commodity. And make "clients never pay" explicit, because every incumbent charges the client and at least some therapists already object to that in public ([Stressless Therapist on Mentaya](https://www.stresslesstherapist.com/mentaya-in-therapy-practices/)). The geographic corollary is that the out-of-network pool concentrates where insurance acceptance is lowest, DC at 41%, Tennessee at 51.5% and California at 55.4% ([Zhu et al. 2024](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11412241/)), and California is also an all-party-consent state, so the richest territory carries the heaviest consent burden.

## In four of five practices the payer, user and decider are one person

Private behavioral-health practice is overwhelmingly solo. Four independent surveys from 2023 to 2026 put the solo share between 75% and 87%: **86.5% of Heard's clients are sole practitioners** ([Heard 2026](https://www.joinheard.com/resources/the-heard-2026-financial-state-of-private-practice-report)), 75% of private-practice psychologists in the APA survey ([APA 2025 Topline](https://www.apa.org/pubs/reports/practitioner/2025/topline-data-tables.pdf)), 82% of a peer-reviewed private-practice sample ([PMC13175211](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13175211/)) and 80% of the SimplePractice/Heard well-being study ([BusinessWire, Oct 2026](https://financialcontent.com/article/bizwire-2026-10-6-new-simplepractice-and-heard-study-independent-behavioral-health-clinicians-find-deep-meaning-in-their-work-but-42-report-burnout)). For those clinicians there is no procurement step; the evaluation is a free trial run in their own practice.

Budget is not the constraint. Heard's data puts median solo revenue at $75,000 and median practice expenses at $18,000, with software already one of the top three expense categories ([Heard 2026](https://www.joinheard.com/resources/the-heard-2026-financial-state-of-private-practice-report)); $600 a year is 0.75% of median revenue and less than the $35–$40 add-on the EHRs already sell. Psychiatrists at **$331,000 average compensation** ([Becker's on Medscape 2026](https://www.beckersbehavioralhealth.com/finance/psychiatrist-pay-falls-despite-broader-physician-compensation-growth-5-notes/)) will not notice the price at all. What constrains the purchase is trust, consent coverage and whether the clinician sees a return inside the trial.

The influencers that matter are external. There is no published data on which channels drive therapist software adoption, but the structure is visible: r/therapists has about **162,000 members and grew 46% in a year** ([GummySearch](https://gummysearch.com/r/therapists/)), researchers recruit therapists through "professional listservs, regional Facebook groups, and emailed national directories" ([PMC13175211](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13175211/)), and the practice-building podcasts carry EHR sponsorships, which means the vendors already believe those shows convert. The community is primed to ask who owns a company: **78% of therapists say platform ownership matters and 85% would refuse one with a large insurer stake** ([PsiAN](https://www.psian.org/practice-management-companies)). The client is a passive influencer who rarely asks for reimbursement help but responds sharply to cost: in a 58,162-patient telepsychiatry study, clients paying more than $40 out of pocket at the first visit were **more than twice as likely to drop out early** ([Scientific Archives](https://www.scientificarchives.com/article/therapeutic-alliance-and-affordability-:-indicators-of-early-dropout-in-telepsychiatry)).

The go-to-market implications follow directly. Ferry sells the way a consumer app sells: self-serve sign-up, a trial that proves itself, and word of mouth in peer communities, podcasts and consultant networks rather than a sales team. Because the decision-maker is also the user, the trial must show a note the clinician trusts and, ideally, a claim moving; the researchers note that insurers take weeks to pay, so a seven-day trial is shorter than any competitor's 14–30 days and may end before the claims half of the product has shown anything. In groups, the owner can buy but cannot make associates consent, so group adoption should be expected to land seat by seat and priced accordingly. In psychiatry, the assistant who batches superbills today is the person whose worst recurring task Ferry removes, which makes them the natural champion. Ownership and data-use disclosure belong on the pricing page, not in the terms, because this community will ask. And cash-pay therapists spend their extra revenue on marketing ([Heard 2026](https://www.joinheard.com/resources/the-heard-2026-financial-state-of-private-practice-report)), so "your clients get money back, so you can say yes when a prospect asks if you take insurance" competes for the marketing budget, which is the line they are already growing.

## Conclusion

The sizing changes what kind of company Ferry is. A $48 million serviceable market at $50 a month is a focused, winnable niche, not a venture-scale story on its own; the way to make it larger is not to inflate the clinician count but to earn more per clinician once the loop is proven, and the top-down wallet shows room, since the pair of products Ferry replaces costs about $950 a year and Ferry asks $600. The claims cross-check is the finding the team did not have before: only about one in five sessions delivered by out-of-network clinicians currently reaches a commercial insurer as a claim, which means the filing promise is a sharp hook for a minority of PPO-heavy, metro, doctoral and psychiatric practices and background value for everyone else. That argues for leading the message with the scribe and the appeal letter, which every clinician uses every day, and letting free filing be the reason the clinician picks this scribe rather than the reason they sign up.

The two numbers to resolve before the beta are internal, not market facts: the real cost of recording a session on the chosen transcription service, which at list price exceeds the subscription several times over, and the trial-to-paid conversion rate in a seven-day window that is shorter than any competitor's and too short for a claim to pay. Everything else in this report, including the gaps below, moves the answer by tens of percent; those two could move it by a multiple.
